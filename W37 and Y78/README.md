# Introduction
Although I have successfully produced a high-affinity binder for the Y248 pocket, designing de novo binders for the W37 (Subdomain 1α) and Y78 (Subdomain 1β) sites was very challenging. Despite several rounds of design and optimisation, I could not obtain stable and reliable binding poses for both the sites. The ipTM scores remained around 0.4 and lower, showing that the models had low confidence in the predicted interactions.
# Limitation
The main problem here is may be due to structural nature of these binding sites rather than a limitation of the AI algorithms alone. Unlike Y248, W37 and Y78 are relatively shallow and flat surface regions. This makes them much harder to target.
De novo design methods like RFdiffusion generally work well when there is a defined pocket or groove where a binder can fit. In these cases, the target provides enough structural features to guide the design. However, W37 and Y78 do not offer the same level of structural support. Because the surfaces are relatively flat, the designed binders often sit on top of them rather than forming a close and stable interaction. This can result in poor shape matching and the loss of important interactions with the surrounding solvent, making the binding interface less stable.

# Troubleshooting
I tried several approaches to improve the binding of these designs, but none produced a convincing result. First, I used guiding potentials like ROG and interface contact constraints to encourage the binders to make more contact with the target. This helped bring the designed proteins closer to the binding sites and increased the buried surface area. However, the resulting structures often had serious problems. In some models, I observed as many as 92 atomic clashes, along with no salt bridges. This suggested that the binders were being pushed against the target surface without forming meaningful chemical interactions. So increasing the number of contacts did not necessarily improve the quality of the binding interface. I also tried using the Complex_beta model to generate beta-sheet-based binders. The idea was to create flat structures that could better match the shape of the W37 and Y78 surfaces. Although the model successfully generated flat beta-sheet backbones, the predicted binding confidence remained low, with an ipTM score of around 0.37. This suggests that simply matching the overall shape of the target surface was not enough. The binding sites may require specific loop arrangements and local structural features that a relatively rigid beta-sheet scaffold could not provide.

Another approach involved partial diffusion, which I used to improve models with severe atomic clashes. By applying low levels of noise, I allowed the backbone to adjust and relax around the target. This helped remove many of the steric clashes and improved the ipTM score from around 0.1 to approximately 0.4. But the improvement was limited. Although the structures became more physically reasonable, they still lacked strong interactions such as hydrogen bonds and salt bridges. The predicted binding remained weak and non-specific. The PAE plots from AlphaFold also continued to show low confidence in the relative positioning of the binder and target, suggesting that the binding pose was still unreliable.
# Results
The W37 and Y78 sites are naturally low-affinity carbohydrate-binding regions. For a newly designed protein to bind successfully, it must form enough favourable interactions to compensate for the loss of flexibility that occurs when it attaches to the target. This includes interactions such as hydrogen bonds, salt bridges and van der Waals contacts. However, on a shallow and relatively flat surface, it is difficult to form enough of these interactions. As a result, the binder may lose much of its conformational freedom without gaining enough binding energy in return. This makes the interaction unstable and helps explain why the models consistently struggled to achieve high binding confidence. These results suggest that designing small proteins from scratch may not be the best approach for targeting the W37 and Y78 sites. Instead, future work could focus on using existing protein scaffolds that are already stable and have well-defined structures. DARPins (Designed Ankyrin Repeat Proteins) and Affibodies are two possible options. Rather than designing an entire protein from scratch, I could use these established scaffolds and modify their binding regions to improve their interaction with the target. Their structural stability may provide a better starting point for targeting relatively flat surfaces.
